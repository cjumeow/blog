I haven't built up much over the years, and I often feel like I've been wasting time. So I want to set myself a goal: write one article every day, recording my thoughts on technology and how the world is evolving, or maybe some technical posts. Writing is my way of telling myself I'm still here, still alive in this world.

My recent realizations, roughly:
1. Whatever I try, I fail — my execution is simply not strong enough. This has been the year AI agents grew exponentially, yet I still haven't attempted any sufficiently hard projects. Meanwhile I keep seeing people online claiming to hit hundreds of thousands of dollars in MRR through "vibe coding." I personally believe it's possible, but it depends entirely on how strong someone's market insight is. Even if an agent can now do in minutes what used to take months and dozens of people, the precondition is still that we, as humans (the creators), need to know which problems are actually worth solving. The truly hard part is judging what's valuable.
2. AI technology is iterating so fast that it's started to paralyze me and make me avoidant — subconsciously I feel like learning anything no longer matters. But in some sense that just reveals I can't adapt to technological change, and that I don't really have the capacity for independent thinking, which makes it easy for me to be swayed by whatever information or opinions I come across. I think in this era, for software, the one certainty is this: we no longer need to learn every framework's configuration and syntax — AI can generate that boilerplate in seconds. But we still genuinely need to learn programming language fundamentals and what good software architecture looks like, so we're able to quickly understand our own codebase and, through that, improve its maintainability and security.
Another thing I'm fairly sure of: now that code is cheap, we increasingly need the ability to quickly build prototypes to test whether a product idea gets positive feedback in the market. And that ability probably rests on whether a developer has a good enough agentic workflow and a good enough sense of product. SaaS in the future might really become a winner-take-all game — the niche markets we used to talk about could get carved up by a flood of AI products. So even if you're going after some niche market, you have to think through two things: 1) does this market actually exist, and 2) are there already a lot of competitors, and if so, can you be ten times better than them? If you can reason clearly through these two questions, you can save yourself a huge amount of time building something nobody wants.
3. Sometimes after reading about someone's success story, I subconsciously want to imitate their traits — but that's a mistake. Whatever the key factor behind their success was, I have no way of knowing their background over the past several years: how many times they failed before succeeding, how many times their company pivoted. Deliberately imitating them just quietly drains my attention, and in the end I lose track of what I'm actually doing.
4. AI is advancing so fast that one way to fight this anxiety is to turn every idea into a methodology that can be quantified and observed.
